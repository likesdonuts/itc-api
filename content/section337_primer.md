## What a Section 337 investigation is

Section 337 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (19 U.S.C. § 1337) makes it unlawful to import goods that infringe a U.S. patent, trademark or copyright, or that involve other unfair acts such as trade secret misappropriation. The U.S. International Trade Commission (the ITC, or "the Commission"), an independent federal agency, investigates complaints under it. Most investigations are about patents.

The remedy is not money. If the Commission finds a violation, it orders the infringing products kept out of the United States, and U.S. Customs and Border Protection enforces that order at the border.

## How it differs from a district court patent case

- **Speed.** An investigation is scheduled to finish in about 16 to 18 months, set by a "target date." District court patent cases usually take longer.
- **No damages, no jury.** The case is tried to an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ), and the outcome is an order excluding products, not a damages award.
- **Jurisdiction over products.** The Commission acts on the imported goods themselves, so it can reach foreign manufacturers that would be hard to sue in a district court.
- **A third party in the room.** A staff attorney from the Commission's Office of Unfair Import Investigations (OUII, or "Staff") often takes part as an independent party representing the public interest.
- **Domestic industry.** The complainant must show it has a "domestic industry" in the United States relating to the patent (see below). A district court plaintiff need not.
- **Parallel cases.** Complainants often file a district court case too. A respondent can have the court case stayed while the ITC case runs (28 U.S.C. § 1659). The Commission's patent findings do not bind the district court.

## What the complainant has to prove

- **Importation.** The accused products are imported into, sold for importation into, or sold in the United States after importation.
- **Infringement.** The imported products infringe at least one valid, enforceable claim of the asserted patents. Claims are the numbered sentences at the end of a patent that define what it covers; a case is usually argued claim by claim.
- **Domestic industry.** Two parts. The *technical prong*: the complainant (or a licensee) makes products that practice the patent. The *economic prong*: significant U.S. investment in plant and equipment, significant employment of labor or capital, or substantial investment in exploiting the patent, such as engineering, research and development, or licensing. An industry that is still being established can qualify.

## Common defenses

- **Non-infringement**: the products do not do what the claims require, often turning on what a claim's words mean ("claim construction").
- **Invalidity**: the claims should not have been granted, because earlier products or publications ("prior art") already disclosed or made obvious the invention, or because the patent does not describe or claim it clearly enough.
- **Unenforceability**, for example inequitable conduct before the Patent Office.
- **No domestic industry**, or **no importation**.
- **License or exhaustion**: the respondent is licensed, or bought the product from someone who was.
- **Redesigns**: a changed product said to avoid the claims, which the Commission may be asked to rule on.

## How a case moves

1. **Complaint.** The complainant files a complaint with exhibits, usually naming several respondents and patents.
2. **Institution.** The Commission votes on whether to start an investigation, normally within 30 days (19 CFR 210.10). Its notice of institution, published in the Federal Register, fixes the scope: the patents and claims, the products, and the parties.
3. **Answers.** Each respondent answers the complaint and notice within 20 days of service (19 CFR 210.13), denying the allegations and stating its defenses. A respondent that does not answer can be found in default.
4. **Before the ALJ.** Discovery, often claim construction (a "Markman" ruling), expert reports, and motions. The ALJ sets a procedural schedule and the target date (within 45 days of institution; 19 CFR 210.51).
5. **Summary determination.** A party can ask the ALJ to decide an issue without a hearing when the facts are not in dispute (19 CFR 210.18). A grant is issued as an *initial determination* (ID), which the Commission may review. A denial sends the issue to the hearing.
6. **Hearing and final ID.** After a trial-like evidentiary hearing, the ALJ issues a final initial determination on whether there is a violation, with a recommended determination on remedy and bond.
7. **Commission review.** Parties petition for review. The Commission decides whether to review all, part or none of the final ID; unreviewed findings become the Commission's own. It then issues its final determination and any remedy by the target date, often with an opinion explaining its reasons.
8. **Presidential review.** The President (in practice, the U.S. Trade Representative) has 60 days to disapprove an exclusion order for policy reasons. Imports may continue during that period under a bond set by the Commission. Disapproval is rare.
9. **Appeal.** A party can appeal the Commission's final determination to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## How cases end early

- **Settlement or license**: the complainant and a respondent agree, and the ALJ terminates the investigation as to that respondent.
- **Consent order**: a respondent agrees to stop importing infringing products, without admitting a violation.
- **Withdrawal**: the complainant withdraws some claims, patents or respondents, or the whole complaint.
- **Default**: a respondent that does not take part can have an exclusion order entered against it without a trial on the merits.
- **Summary determination** can decide the whole case, for example that there is no domestic industry.

## Remedies

- **Limited exclusion order (LEO)**: bars infringing products of the named respondents from entry.
- **General exclusion order (GEO)**: bars all infringing products, whoever makes them. It requires more: for example, a pattern of violation where the sources are hard to identify, or a risk that an LEO would be circumvented.
- **Cease and desist order (CDO)**: stops a respondent from selling infringing products it has already imported and holds in U.S. inventory.
- Before ordering a remedy, the Commission weighs the **public interest**: public health and welfare, competition, the supply of like products in the United States, and U.S. consumers.

## Terms you will see

- **ALJ**: Administrative Law Judge, who runs the case until the final ID.
- **ID / Final ID**: initial determination. Non-final IDs decide things along the way (terminations, summary determinations, target dates) and become the Commission's own unless it reviews them. The final ID decides whether there is a violation.
- **RD**: recommended determination on remedy and bond, issued with the final ID.
- **OUII / Staff**: the Commission's Office of Unfair Import Investigations.
- **Target date**: the date by which the Commission aims to finish.
- **Complainant / Respondent**: the party that filed the complaint / a party accused of the violation.
- **Public version**: filings and decisions are often issued confidentially first, then publicly with business information redacted. Only public versions are read here.
